01 Introduction

Background & motivation

FOURIER IMAGE LAB was inspired by Chapter 6 of Signals and Systems, second edition, by Alan V. Oppenheim, Alan S. Willsky, and S. Hamid Nawab. Its discussion of magnitude and phase prompted a question: how can frequency-domain ideas become something we can observe and manipulate in an image?

The lab extends that motivation into interactive visual experiments. Change an input, observe a reconstruction, and connect the result to the mathematics. It is a tool for exploration alongside the original textbook; the five experiments are developed for this site rather than presented as reproductions of the chapter.

Textbook reference: Oppenheim, Willsky, and Nawab. Signals and Systems, 2nd edition, Prentice Hall, © 1997. Chapter 6: Time- and Frequency Characterization of Signals and Systems. Publisher and chapter contents ↗

02 Mathematical Background

The lab uses a square grid with N=256. Here x,y are pixel coordinates and u,v are discrete frequency indices, all initially indexed from 0 to N-1. The horizontal coordinate is first; image rows increase downward.

2D Discrete Fourier Transform

For one real-valued image channel I(x,y), the forward transform is

\begin{aligned}F(u,v)&=\sum_{y=0}^{N-1}\sum_{x=0}^{N-1} I(x,y)\\&\qquad\cdot e^{-i2\pi(ux+vy)/N}.\end{aligned}

The FFT is an efficient way to compute this DFT. The forward transform is unnormalized. RGB experiments apply it independently to each color channel; Spectrum / Translation uses a grayscale channel.

Magnitude and phase

F(u,v)=|F(u,v)|e^{i\phi(u,v)}

Magnitude |F| is the amplitude of a frequency component. Phase \phi=\operatorname{atan2}(\operatorname{Im}F,\operatorname{Re}F) describes its phase relative to the coordinate origin. Their combination determines the reconstruction; phase alone does not contain all image information.

At zero magnitude, phase is undefined. The code stores zero as a numerical convention. Spectrum / Translation masks zero or near-zero magnitudes in neutral gray when displaying phase; that mask does not remove coefficients from the reconstruction.

Inverse Fourier Transform

For an edited spectrum G(u,v), the inverse is

\begin{aligned}I'(x,y)&=\frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{v=0}^{N-1}\sum_{u=0}^{N-1}G(u,v)\\&\qquad\cdot e^{i2\pi(ux+vy)/N}.\end{aligned}

The IFFT divides by N in each dimension, giving the factor 1/N^2. The transform assumes periodic boundaries.

Frequency coordinates

Native FFT arrays place DC (zero frequency) at index [0, 0]. For an axis index u, the signed bin is k_u=u below N/2, otherwise k_u=u-N. At 256 samples, the signed range is −128…127 bins, and k_u/N is frequency in cycles/pixel. The axial Nyquist frequency has magnitude 0.5 cycles/pixel.

fftshift centers display copies without moving the coefficients used in calculations. Frequency units and display conventions specific to a filter or mask are explained on its experiment page.

Real images and Hermitian symmetry

F((-u)\bmod N,(-v)\bmod N)=\overline{F(u,v)}

Real-valued inputs have conjugate frequency pairs. The shared RGB analysis projects tiny floating-point errors onto this symmetry before extracting magnitude and phase. Reconstruction keeps the self-conjugate DC / Nyquist points real. Experiment 004 uses symmetric binary masks so replacing phase preserves this relation.
